Reform and Mutschler were originaltwo different manufacturerswith a focus on fountain pens from Heidelberg.

1919becameReformfounded near Heidelberg by Ludwig Jung. He poached two Kaweco employees who became co-owners of Reform.
In 1922 Reform moved to Heidelberg and in 1928 to Nieder-Ramstadt.

1929becameMutschlerfounded by other former Kaweco employees, with Philipp Mutschler as owner. Shortly before, Kaweco had gone bankrupt.

Reform was dissolved in 1956. Mutschler took over the brand name, while another manufacturer, ERO, took over Reform's branch.

Mutschler, still in Heidelberg, moved to Dossenheimer Landstrasse 100 in the same city in 1973. In a sense it is a return to the beginning, there at this one Address formerly manufactured by Kaweco.

Around this time, Mutschler probably also produced four-color ballpoint pens for Pelikan and Geha.
At least that oneWhen C4LFour-color ballpoint pen is probably from Reform, as a version with 'Reform' inscription exists.

After another move in 1999, the Mutschler company was finally given up at the beginning of the 21st century.

Reform fountain pen factory (before Mutschler)

Author: Volker Teutschländer, January 18, 2012 Archiving the inactive website http://www.muehltal-odenwald.de/

Fountain pens and ballpoint pens are still emblazoned on the facade of the former “Reform” office building - this is what local people still call the long-converted buildings today. They are reminiscent of the heyday of the penmanufactures in the northwestern Odenwald, among which “the reform” in Nieder-Ramstadt used to play a leading role. The addition of “ERO” on the advertising device draws attention to the fact that fountain pen production is history in the “reform” – yes, even in its successor company ERO.
In 2010, Inge Wegt née Jung, the last daughter of one of the company founders who moved their company from Heidelberg to Nieder-Ramstadt in 1928, died. Her father, the businessman Ludwig Jung, the wood turner Paul Fortran and the wood turner Johann Heinz founded a company for the production of “safety fountain pens” in St. Ilgen near Heidelberg in 1919.
After the Reichenbach and Bessunger match factory on Ober-Ramstädter Straße in Nieder-Ramstadt closed, the three entrepreneurs took over the properties in 1928 and moved the business to Nieder-Ramstadt. Under the “Reform” brand they manufactured fountain pens, ink pens and mechanical pencils from simple to luxury versions, most of which were exported.
Production was carried out on simple lathes and turret lathes. External contours were also sanded later. A fountain pen consisted of 26 individual parts. Hard rubber, galalith and hardened celluloid were used as raw materials according to a patent from the company Dietz and Böttcher from the Hahnmühle near Pfungstadt. One employee produced around 120 pieces a week.
It was not delivered through wholesalers, but rather representatives promoted the export of the “Worldpen” throughout Europe, Central America and Asia. 99 percent of the products went abroad. In individual countries, the market leadership of the “reform” amounted to a monopoly position. A branch was established in Alsace. The “reform” was far less well known in Germany than on the world market. In 1941 the “Reform fountain pen factory Gmbh” was renamed “Reform fountain pen factory Heinz & Jung” and Fortran was apparently no longer one of the owners. Around this time, the company had reached its highest level of employment with around 100 workers and employees.
Thomas Neureither (Heidelberg), who deals with fountain pen production in and around Heidelberg, is also an expert on the Nieder-Ramstadt reform based on two writings on the company's history and extensive inquiries with Inge Wegt. Neureither has enriched the limited knowledge known here at the site of the former reform with some important additions, including:
"The global economic crisis made itself felt in Europe with a delay. According to an economic paper from 1950, 1932 was the worst sales year for the fountain pen industry. The devaluation of the English pound and the establishment of massive protective tariffs made international trade more difficult, and the massive rise in gold prices forced a change in pen production. However, exports were able to be changed and domestic business increased noticeably. In the wake of rearmament, considerable quantities of fountain pens were also required. The demand The number of writing instruments was so high that orders from the population could only be fulfilled to a limited extent, if at all. Although there was a massive shortage of raw materials in 1943, fountain pen production in Germany was even higher in 1945 than in 1936!
Neureither is aware that, contrary to what is often said today, outstanding successes were achieved immediately after the war. "In fact, after the war there was a phase of upheaval with a reorientation. However, the demand for writing instruments did not decline worldwide and the large number of new companies founded after the war shows the stability of this branch of the economy. One problem was certainly the return of production to peacetime conditions. I have an inquiry from a business in Regensburg from 1943, whose order had to be rejected by the reform due to "operational change". This meant at that time that Speer's forced management of the plant was had converted to arms production.”
Because of the company's unusually good international reputation, the “reform” was given credit worldwide; the export goods were often paid for in advance with the order. According to an article, a partial copy of which is available to Neureither, up to 95 percent of foreign deliveries were paid for in advance. The reform was even able to claim that it was the first company in Germany to receive a non-cancelable letter of credit from abroad after the war. Almost all payment transactions with foreign countries were “currently (probably 1952)” based on this basis.
For this description of the successful, although (in Nieder-Raumstadt) not even thirty-year history of reform, Thomas Neureither has provided images of two production machines from his equipment park, a Wolff-Jahn lathe and a guilloche machine (read: Gilluschier machine). Neureither: "A delicate scratch pattern could be applied to the otherwise plain surface of a black hard rubber filler, which also made the writing instrument easier to grip. With this machine, small industrial diamonds transferred a pattern coded in a mechanical disk. Guilloche was named after its inventor, the French mechanic and goldsmith Guillot. Originally, tobacco tins or pocket watch lids were decorated with a grain or cycloid pattern. This also shows the love and precision with which it was done Fountain pens were made in the 20s and 30s and were often kept by their owners like pieces of jewelry.”
"The end of the reform in 1956 was actually quite strange: Ludwig Jung refused to exchange the craftsmanship of his products for the production of mass-produced injection-molded parts, which was still questionable at the time, and preferred to close the company, which could no longer be maintained on this basis. In fact, for a long time there was a tacit agreement between the producers not to use the injection molding machines, which had been in existence since 1938, for fountain pen production. In addition to the feared uprisings of the... Factory workers who would have been threatened with losing their jobs had they not wanted to destroy the world reputation of German quality fillers. In particular, the pressed threads were - and still are to this day - the hand-cut threads.
The Reform fountain pens offered on eBay - some of them unused - mostly refer to Reform fountain pens from Heidelberg. The brand was sold to the Mutschler-Schreibgeräte company, which continued to use the trade name and the winged 'R' until its own bankruptcy. These were good everyday fountain pens, but - no comparison with the Nieder-Ramstadt celluloid fountain pens and their gold nibs.”
In 1958, the Rodenhäuser company from Ober-Ramstadt “Ero” took over the Reform properties and moved their writing instrument production here.
