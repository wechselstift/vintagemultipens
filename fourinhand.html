The Four-in-Hand is one of the most famous old four-color pencils.
Its distribution area was primarily Great Britain, Germany, and France.

It has a very beautiful, antique look and was produced in a wide variety of variations (lacquering, guilloche, coating).
Because it was produced in such large quantities, it is still very cheap today and easy to find with its original box.
But no one has written about his background yet. I would like to change that.

There are 2 versions of the Four-in-Hand.



The first version appeared in the mid to late 1920s,
the second in the early to mid-1930s.

The 2nd version cost Johann Faber 0.75 Reichsmark each in 1939, which is around 5 euros in 2026 according to the currency calculator.

Construction

The sleeve containing the writing opening is surrounded by an outer second sleeve corresponding to a long sliding ring. The latter has a driver with whom she When advancing, depending on the rotation of the sleeves relative to each other, another lead holder protruding through the longitudinal slots from the inner sliding sleeve transported to the writing opening and held in the writing position by friction.

Service

Both pens basically work the same:
The two halves of the pen are pulled apart as far as possible. This can require a lot of force with old pens if they haven't been used for a long time.
You should see a color indicator on the front sleeve. Attention: There is a 2-color version of the same mechanism that does not have indicators.
The pin barrels are twisted until a dent or bump on the outer half lines up with one of the paint dots on the inner barrel.
Now the sleeves are pushed together. This pushes the mine forward.
The feed is stepless. Once the lead has been written off, the halves are pushed together an additional piece of any size.

Sliding pins without a locking mechanism were the norm at the time, but in most cases the pushing only served to activate the actual feed mechanism, which goes through an ordinary rotating mechanism was designed on each lead holder to bring it into the writing position.

Name origin

“Four-in-hand” is a British tie knot that is still widely used today. According to one theory, this had its origins in the Four-in-Hand coaching club in London, which in turn had this name because four-in-hand driving involved driving four horses with four reins in the same hand.
The pen was probably named based on this phrase - since you hold four colors in your hand.

The pen is also known as "Foursome" (England, 2nd version), "Quatricolor" (France, 2nd version), "Penkala Quartett" (Germany, 1st version).
The 2nd version was also developed by A.W. without its own product name. Faber-Castell and Johann Faber sold.

Geographical origin

The inventors are a Czech and an Austrian from 1924. But they assigned the rights to an English company. It can be assumed that the pens have certain requirements The patents of the inventor duo were protected, even if, as is the case with most pens, they were never depicted or/and described almost identically in the patent.

Similar patents followed from other inventors:
1926 an English patent in which a pen like the 1st version is described and shown.
1927 a German patent which also describes and depicts the same version.
In the 1930s, when the 2nd version appeared, the same German company published a patent that described and depicted the 2nd version - however with an improvement.

However, the patent numbers in the instructions point to the patent of the inventor duo for both versions. Both once in the English and once in the German product list.

However, despite the English company's rights to the Four-in-Hand, not all Four-in-Hands appear to have been manufactured in England.
Some of the first versions say "GERMANY".
On some of the 2nd version you can find "FOREIGN". One of the packaging says “Boxed in England”, which also suggests an import.

My hypothesis is that the first version was mainly produced under license in Germany. The political tensions of the 1930s prevented the production of version 2 possibly relocated to England for the English (and perhaps French) market?
